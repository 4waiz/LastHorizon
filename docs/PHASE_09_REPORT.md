# Phase 9 report — optional adult action systems, crime, and an original Heat model

**Status: the optional systems are in, gated at eighteen, and the main story
still ends without anybody being hurt.** Four weapons, eleven crimes, a Heat
scale from 0 to 5 built on a single belief with three writers, nine officer
behaviours, arrest, impound, surrender and release. What is *not* done is in §9
and §10 rather than rounded away: officers walk rather than path, four of the
eleven crimes have no trigger in the world yet, roadblocks are counted but never
placed, and there is no patrol car model behind a motorised pursuit.

**Date:** 2026-08-10
**Base:** `phase-08-story`
**Gate:** `npm run verify` green — **1,363 unit tests**, up from 1,355;
**111 Playwright scenarios**, up from 92
**Branch:** `phase-06-population`

---

## 1. The tone decision came before the systems

The brief asks for firearms and police in a game whose whole register is a
calm village at dusk. Those two things do not automatically fit together, and
the way most games resolve it is with a health bar and a red screen.

This one does not have a health bar. It has **composure**, a 0..1 number per
person that falls when they are hit and recovers on its own. At zero an NPC is
*incapacitated* — they sit down, they stop walking their schedule, and a clinic
visit restores them. There is no blood, no gore, no dismemberment and no injury
detail anywhere in the phase, because there is nothing in the model to render:
a shot moves a number and plays a small dust puff, and the strongest word the
code uses for the result is "sat down".

That decision is upstream of everything else here. A shove costs 0.34
composure, so three shoves end an encounter without a weapon existing at all,
and every police encounter has a non-lethal exit by construction rather than by
authoring.

## 3. Criterion 2 is a property of two methods, not a promise

*"Do not make police omniscient"* is the kind of requirement that is easy to
agree with and hard to keep, because every frame offers a chance to read the
player's transform and be done with it. So it was made structurally impossible
rather than carefully avoided:

**`HeatSystem` has one belief field and exactly three writers.**
`officerSaw` (an officer watched it happen), `deliverReport` (a witness reached
help), and `findEvidence` (an officer walked over something that was left
behind). There is no fourth. And `advance(dt, officerPositions)` **does not take
the player's position** — it could not cheat if it wanted to, because it does
not have the number.

**`OfficerCorps.sees(id)` is the only place an officer learns where the player
is.** It applies the same four gates every shopkeeper got in Phase 6 — range,
indoors, occlusion against the real collision proxy, and nothing else. An
officer facing a wall is as blind as anybody else facing a wall.

Everything else follows. Officers spawn on a ring **around the belief**, and
`spawn(near)` takes one argument, so it cannot spawn near the player. A belief
goes stale after 22 seconds and stops being worth investigating. Heat decays
when nothing refreshes it, which is why hiding works.

The end-to-end tests check this from the outside rather than trusting the
argument: an unwitnessed, evidence-free crime raises nothing at all; a report
from one side of the village leaves the belief there while the player walks to
the other; and every spawned officer is more than ten metres from the player
and walking toward the report rather than toward them.

## 4. Six bugs, and where each was found

### Bloom decay did nothing at all — *unit test*

Documented as "the fraction of bloom shed per second", implemented as an
absolute subtraction. At 0.9 rad/s it removed 0.012 of bloom between shots,
which is to say a player firing on full automatic accumulated spread forever.
`bloomValue *= max(0, 1 - decay * dt)` — the thing the comment always said.

### A single large `advance` wiped freshly-raised Heat — *unit test*

`advance(30)` delivered a queued report and then charged thirty seconds of
decay against the Heat it had just raised, so a witness who reached help during
a long step was worth nothing. Fixed with `beliefTouched` and `heatRaised`
flags that stop same-tick decay, which is also the correct behaviour for the
normal 16 ms case.

### An officer arrested a driver through the car window — *unit test*

`doWarn` reached `ARREST_RANGE` and took hold of somebody sitting in a running
car. It now begins a pursuit instead when `playerDriving`. This turned out to
matter a great deal in §5.

### An officer re-investigated the same cold report forever — *unit test*

Nothing checked belief age before deciding to investigate, so a report from
four minutes ago sent officers back to the same empty corner indefinitely.
`BELIEF_STALE_SECONDS` now gates it.

### Surrendering was free — *Playwright*

**This is the one the unit tests could never have found, and the reason the
brief asks for browser runs.**

`CombatDirector.surrender()` cleared Heat, retired the squad and posted a
toast. It never called `host.onArrest`. Every consequence of being taken in —
the fade, the four hours, the fine, the impound, the move to the station, the
save — lives inside that one callback, and surrender skipped all of it.

From the inside it looked correct: Heat went to zero, `arrests` incremented,
the officers went away. Three of the four things a unit test would assert were
true. What was actually shipping was an option that dominated every other
option in the game — no cost, no time lost, and the getaway car left running in
the street with nobody in it.

It was found by writing a test for the *impound yard*: surrender in a car, then
go to the desk and try to get the car back. There was no car in the yard. The
fix is one line and a paragraph of comment explaining why it must never be
removed again; `tests/combat.test.ts` now has a `CombatDirector` test that
asserts `onArrest` fires, because that class had no unit coverage at all before
this phase — which is how the gap survived.

### Aiming could not survive a frame — *browser, by eye*

Found by trying to take a screenshot of the aim camera and getting the ordinary
camera back.

`setAiming` on the test bridge wrote straight to `WeaponSystem`. But
`updateCombat` rebuilds aim from `input.aimHeld` every single frame, so the
write was undone on the very next tick:

```
setAiming(true)  -> stance "aiming"
settle(1)        -> stance "drawn"
settle(60)       -> stance "drawn"
```

Exactly the surrender bug's shape — correct if you check it immediately, gone
the moment the frame loop runs — and it meant the aim camera could not be
driven from a test at all. Aiming is a *held* state, so the fix is to hold it:
`InputManager.setAimHeld` joins the mouse and the trigger as a third source,
and the bridge presses that instead of writing past the loop. The same pattern
`setInteractHeld` has used since Phase 3.

### The reticle sat on the back of the character's head — *browser, by eye*

The first aim screenshot pulled the camera in correctly and put the crosshair
squarely on the character. The shoulder bias moved the camera sideways but the
look-at still converged on the player, so widening the bias only orbited the
camera and left them dead centre. Aiming needs the two lines parallel: the
look-at target now slides by the same bias, blended, so the character moves off
to the chosen shoulder and the reticle looks at the world. Both shoulders were
then checked by eye, which is the only way this class of thing gets checked.

### And three tests that were wrong rather than the game

Worth recording, because two of them were wrong in the same direction:
assuming the game should do something it correctly refuses.

1. A pursuit test expected `investigate` before `approach`. An officer who can
   already see somebody has nothing to investigate; the ladder is correct and
   the assertion was not. It was moved to the cold-belief test, where an
   officer genuinely does have somewhere to go and nobody to look at.
2. An impound test expected an officer on foot to arrest a driver. That is the
   bug fixed above, working as intended. The test was rewritten to surrender.
3. An arrest test asserted the *day* clock moved. `setTime` pins it in test
   mode, so it never does; the four hours land on the life clock.

## 5. What Playwright found that nothing else could

The Phase 8 report's lesson was that an end-to-end test which reports by
objective id proves the graph and not the game. Phase 9's version of that
lesson is sharper, and it is worth writing down:

**A synchronous `settle()` loop inside one `page.evaluate` cannot observe any
async host work.** `performArrest` awaits a CSS fade backed by a real
`setTimeout`; a tight `for` loop calling `settle()` never unwinds the stack, so
the continuation never runs, so the fine is never charged and the player is
never moved. The first version of the arrest test failed for exactly this
reason and looked *identical* to a missing feature — no cash taken, no time
passed, nobody moved.

`settleThrough()` in `tests/e2e/combat.spec.ts` drives frames *and* yields to
the event loop between them, and carries a comment saying why. Any future test
that waits on a fade, a save or a zone load needs it.

## 6. Against the acceptance criteria

**1. The player cannot access firearms before eighteen.**
Held in three independent places: `WeaponSystem.acquire` refuses,
`WeaponSystem.restore` re-applies the gate to a loaded save, and the police
desk's three ammunition offers carry `minAge: 18`. The test bridge deliberately
routes through `acquire` rather than around it, so a bridge call cannot arm a
minor either. Proved on the real life clock — the test ages a fifteen-year-old
to eighteen and watches the answer change — rather than against a stubbed age.

**2. The police never know without a valid information path.** §3. One belief
field, three writers, no player position in `advance`, one sight method with
four gates. Checked from the outside by three browser scenarios.

**3. No main-story completion requires killing or graphic violence.** The
fourteen objective kinds include `combat`, and **no main-story quest uses it** —
`scripts/check-story.mjs` walks all fifteen main quests and would fail if one
did. The criminal route through chapter 6 is theft and evasion, not violence.
There is no death in the model at all: composure floors at zero and recovers.

**4. No child NPC can be targeted or participate in combat.** Two independent
refusals. `Game.shotTargets()` excludes the child age band when it builds the
target list, and `Ballistics.traceShot` refuses any target whose `targetable`
is false regardless of who put it there. A catalogue mistake cannot become a
targetable child, because it would have to defeat both.

**5. Arrest advances time, costs money or reputation, moves the player and
vehicle safely, and never corrupts a quest or save.** `Game.performArrest` does
them in a fixed order — fade, impound the ridden vehicle *before* moving the
player, advance four hours, charge the fine, place them outside the station,
**then** save — so every mutation has settled before anything is written. Both
routes into it (caught, and surrendered) are exercised in the browser, wallet
and life clock included.

**6. The player has a non-lethal route through every police encounter.**
Surrender is always available while wanted, and now costs what being caught
costs. Hiding works because Heat decays on a cold trail. Unarmed shove ends a
civilian encounter in three hits. And the composure model has no lethal state
to route around in the first place.

## 7. What a peak police response costs

Measured with Chrome DevTools MCP against the production preview build, 1x CPU,
no network throttling. The scenario is the worst one the systems can produce:
Heat held at 5, the player driving so nobody can end it with an arrest, three
officers turned out with one of them motorised, on top of the ordinary village
population of 26 bodies.

| | mean | p50 | p95 | p99 | worst |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Heat 5, 3 officers, vehicle pursuit | 15.14 ms | 16.6 ms | 25.0 ms | 29.2 ms | **58.3 ms** |
| Same car, same place, Heat 0 | 15.34 ms | 16.6 ms | 25.0 ms | 29.1 ms | 29.2 ms |

**A full police response costs nothing measurable in steady state.** Mean, p50,
p95 and p99 are identical to the baseline inside sampling noise — the pursuit
run is marginally *faster* on the mean, which is noise rather than a result.
That is what the design predicted: an officer is a pedestrian body plus a state
machine that does a distance check, a ray and a `Math.hypot`, and Phase 6
already paid for the body.

The one real number is the **58.3 ms worst frame**, against a 29.2 ms worst in
the baseline. It is a spawn hitch — registering an officer's geometry with the
renderer the first time one is drawn — and it happens once per officer, not per
frame. It is a hitch worth fixing (a warmed-up officer prototype would do it),
but it is a hitch, not a cost.

Draw calls came out at 453 with three officers against 468 with none, which
looks backwards until you notice both runs were sampled at different points in
the traffic and NPC LOD cycle. The honest reading is that three officers are
lost in that variance.

**Caveats, stated rather than buried:** one machine, unthrottled, one run each,
and the sampler measures `requestAnimationFrame` intervals rather than GPU time.
This establishes that the police are not a new performance problem. It does not
replace a budget, and `docs/PERFORMANCE_BUDGETS.md` still governs.

## 8. Budgets

| Budget | Phase 8 | Phase 9 | Limit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| app chunk | 363.2 kB | **379.7 kB** | 390 kB *(was 375)* |
| JS total | 1,103.6 kB | **1,112.4 kB** | 1,120 kB |
| GLB models | 1,280.7 kB | **1,345.8 kB** | 1,360 kB |
| initial load | 4,186.5 kB | **4,199.4 kB** | 4,200 kB |
| shipped total | 7,473.8 kB | **7,588.2 kB** | 7,600 kB |

Lazy: `CombatSubsystem-*.js` at **28.3 kB** and `weapons.glb` at **65.1 kB**,
neither fetched until a weapon is drawn or a crime is committed — which for
every player under eighteen, and most players over it, is never.

**One limit was raised: app chunk 375 → 390 kB.** That is the fourth
consecutive phase to cost the app chunk about 15 kB, and the reason is always
the same — the system goes lazy and its wiring cannot. `OfficerCorps` was
extracted from `Game` mid-phase when the budget failed, which recovered 0.5 kB
and produced a better boundary anyway.

### The initial-load raise was reverted, because the gate was wrong

The first pass raised initial load 4,200 → 4,220 kB as well. Then JS total came
within **0.1 kB** of its own limit, and the rule here is to move something
before raising a ceiling — so rather than raise a third number, the question
became what was actually being counted.

Four chunks were being counted as startup weight that never were:
`TestRoad-`, `VehicleControls-`, `VehicleAccess-` and `VehicleDynamics-`. All
four are `await import(...)` in `Game`, which is why Vite emits them
separately; only two of the five vehicle chunks had ever been listed in
`LAZY_CHUNK_PREFIXES`, and `TestRoad` is behind a feature flag a normal player
cannot reach at all. 7.5 kB.

With them listed, JS total is 1,112.4 kB and initial load is **4,199.4 kB** —
under the limit that had just been declared too small. The raise was reverted.
This is the third phase running in which the budget gate itself was the thing
that needed fixing (`StorySubsystem-` in Phase 8, `CombatSubsystem-` earlier in
this one), which is worth watching: a gate that under-reports headroom causes
exactly the wrong decision.

### Read this before starting Phase 10

**`initial load` has 0.6 kB of headroom.** 4,199.4 against 4,200. That is not
a comfortable margin, it is a tripwire — the next commit that adds a line of
eager code fails the gate, and it will look like that commit's fault.

It is stated here rather than fixed by another raise on purpose. Phase 9 has
already spent the easy answer: the 7.5 kB recovered above was a measurement
error, and there is not a second one waiting. **The next phase's first budget
question is what moves out of the app chunk, not what number goes up.** The
obvious candidate is the vehicle and combat wiring in `Game`, which is now the
largest eager thing in the repository that is not the renderer.

## 9. Remaining risk

**Officers walk in straight lines.** `OfficerCorps.advance` steps toward a goal
and snaps to terrain height. It does not use the navmesh, because the navmesh
belongs to `Population` and an officer is not one of its agents. An officer
will walk into a fence. This is the weakest thing in the phase and it is
deliberate rather than overlooked — it is enough for a pursuit across open
village streets, and wiring officers into the agent system is a larger change
than this phase should carry.

**There is no patrol car.** `hasVehicle` now respects the Heat tier's declared
car count, so `pursue_vehicle` is reachable in the game rather than only in a
unit test, and a motorised officer moves at driving speed. But there is no car
model following them: the player sees somebody on foot keeping up with a
hatchback. This is the one place the phase asks the player to look away.

**Roadblocks are counted and never placed.** `HeatTier.roadblocks` is honoured
by the stats and by nothing else.

**A stationary wanted player is arrested repeatedly.** Found while setting up
the performance profile: at Heat 5, standing still, the run recorded thirteen
arrests. Each one is individually correct — released outside the station, Heat
cleared, fine settled — but nothing stops the next report from arriving
immediately, so it loops. A real player would have to choose to stand there,
and would notice. It still wants a grace period after release.

**One officer spawn costs a 58 ms frame.** §7. Once per officer, not per frame,
but it is visible.

  `weapon_discharge`, `assault`, `theft`, `attack_police` and `escape_arrest`

- **NPC surrender and clinic recovery** exist in the composure model but no NPC

### The suite outgrew a single run, and is now sharded

Running all 111 scenarios in one process crashed the browser at the tail —
`Target crashed`, worker exit `0xC0000409` — 24 minutes in, taking two
story-mode scenarios and eight unrun tests with it. It is not a regression:
`story.spec.ts` passes 11/11 on its own, and those same two tests passed in the
run immediately before Phase 9's scenarios were added.

It is memory. Every scenario boots a real WebGL context, the suite runs
serially in one browser process by design, and eighteen more contexts was the
straw. `playwright.config.ts` has carried a comment since Phase 6 saying "a run
of this size needs splitting rather than tolerating; until then, retry" — and a
retry cannot help here, because the thing that died is the process the retry
would run in.

So CI now shards: `--shard=n/2` across a two-runner matrix per browser, six
jobs instead of three, each with a fresh browser. The comment in
`playwright.config.ts` has been updated to say the split happened rather than
that it is still owed.
